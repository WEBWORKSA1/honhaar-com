How to win student competitions

Honhaar Editorial · Updated October 2026 · 7 min read

Competition wins are proof. A certificate from a judged contest tells a scholarship panel what an adjective never can: someone independent compared your work with others and chose yours.

1. Pick contests you can actually win

Big national olympiads and hackathons have huge fields. Balance them with smaller, focused contests — regional essay prizes, school-level science fairs, niche coding challenges, monthly themes like Honhaar’s talent contest. Five entries in contests with 200 entrants usually beat one entry in a contest with 200,000.

2. Read the rubric like a judge

Most contests publish criteria and weights. Turn them into a checklist and score your own draft honestly before you submit. If “originality” is 35% of the score, a polished but predictable entry will lose to a rougher but genuinely new idea.

3. Answer the theme — exactly

Judges discard entries that drift from the prompt. Put the theme into your first lines (essay), your title and artist note (art), or your problem statement (coding and innovation).

4. Make it specific to your world

An essay about “climate change” is one of thousands; an essay about the pond in your village that dried up in 2024, and what the panchayat did next, is one of one. Specific places, people and numbers make work memorable.

5. Finish early, then improve

Complete a full version a week before the deadline. Spend the last week improving it with feedback from one person who will be honest. Most entries are submitted in the final 24 hours — and it shows.

6. Follow the rules to the letter

  • Word or time limits, file formats and naming rules.
  • Sharing links set to “anyone with the link can view”.
  • Original work only — plagiarism and AI-generated entries are usually disqualified.

7. Turn every entry into portfolio material

Keep a simple portfolio page or folder with each entry, the brief, the result and one line on what you learned. Even non-winning entries show initiative. When a scholarship asks for evidence of talent or leadership, you’ll have it ready.
